Wind correction

The heading to fly to stay on your course, and how fast you will cover the ground.

Winds aloft forecasts give the wind in degrees true, so use your true course from the chart.

True heading—
Groundspeed—
Magnetic heading—
Time en route—
Head / tailwind—
How it works

The wind pushes the airplane sideways, so you point the nose a little into the wind. That angle is the wind correction angle (WCA).

  • WCA = asin( wind speed × sin(wind direction − course) ÷ TAS )
  • True heading = true course + WCA (wind from the right means a right correction)
  • Groundspeed = TAS × cos(WCA) − wind speed × cos(wind direction − course)

Then apply variation to get the magnetic heading: East is least, West is best (subtract east variation, add west). Add compass deviation from the card in the airplane to get compass heading.

On a paper E6B: set the wind direction under the true index, mark the wind speed up from the grommet, turn the true course to the index, slide the TAS under the grommet, and read the WCA off the mark and the groundspeed under the grommet.

Get groundspeed from GPS, or from two checkpoints with the Time, speed, distance tool. Convert the magnetic heading you are holding to true first (East is least, West is best, in reverse).

How it works

The airplane moves through the air on its heading at TAS, and the air itself moves with the wind. What you see on the ground (course and groundspeed) is the two added together, so the wind is the difference between the ground vector and the air vector.

Use it to check the forecast winds: if the real wind is very different, your fuel and time estimates are too.

How it works

Distance = speed × time, so time = distance ÷ speed and speed = distance ÷ time. Speed in knots is nautical miles per hour, so work with time in hours or divide by 60.

Quick mental math: at 120 kt you fly 2 nm a minute, at 90 kt 1.5 nm a minute, at 60 kt 1 nm a minute.

How it works
  • Fuel = burn rate × time
  • Endurance = fuel ÷ burn rate
  • Burn rate = fuel used ÷ time

Avgas weighs about 6 lb per US gallon and Jet A about 6.7 lb. You need the weight for weight and balance.

Plan with the reserve on top: fuel needed for the trip plus the reserve must be no more than your usable fuel.

How it works
  • Vertical speed (fpm) = groundspeed × gradient (ft/nm) ÷ 60
  • One degree is about 106 ft per nm, so a 3° path is about 318 ft/nm.
  • Descent time = altitude to lose ÷ descent rate, and distance = groundspeed × time.

Rules of thumb: on a 3° glidepath, descent rate ≈ groundspeed × 5 (90 kt → about 450 fpm). For a 3° descent, start down about 3 nm for every 1,000 ft to lose.

True airspeed

How fast you are really moving through the air, which is faster than the airspeed indicator shows as you climb.

Pressure altitude is what the altimeter reads set to 29.92 (see Density altitude). Calibrated airspeed is indicated airspeed corrected with the table in the POH.

How it works

The airspeed indicator measures air pressure ramming into the pitot tube. Higher up, the air is thinner, so the same indicated airspeed means you are moving faster. True airspeed corrects for the air's density (from pressure altitude and temperature), and for compressibility at higher speeds.

Rule of thumb: TAS is about 2% higher than CAS for every 1,000 ft of altitude. On a paper E6B, set the temperature over the pressure altitude in the airspeed window, then read TAS on the outer scale over CAS on the inner scale.

Density altitude

The altitude the airplane "feels". Hot, high and low-pressure days make it climb and take off like it is much higher.

Use the altimeter setting and temperature from the ATIS, AWOS or METAR.

Rule of thumb—
How it works
  1. Pressure altitude corrects for non-standard pressure: PA ≈ elevation + (29.92 − altimeter) × 1,000.
  2. Standard temperature is 15 °C at sea level, dropping about 2 °C per 1,000 ft.
  3. Density altitude then corrects for temperature: DA ≈ PA + 120 × (OAT − standard temp).

The big answer uses the exact standard-atmosphere formulas; the rule of thumb is shown so you can check your own math.

Use density altitude with the takeoff, climb and landing charts in your POH. High density altitude means longer takeoff rolls and weaker climbs.

Crosswind

How much of the wind is across the runway and how much is down it.

Tower and ATIS winds are magnetic, like runway numbers. METAR and TAF winds are true; near large variation, convert first.

Crosswind—
Headwind—
How it works
  • Crosswind = wind speed × sin(angle between wind and runway)
  • Headwind = wind speed × cos(angle) (negative means a tailwind)

Clock rule: 15° off the runway ≈ ¼ of the wind is crosswind, 30° ≈ ½, 45° ≈ ¾, 60° or more ≈ all of it.

About the formats
  • Decimal degrees (29.1799, -81.0581): latitude first; south and west are negative.
  • Degrees, decimal minutes (N29° 10.794'): what Garmin units, ForeFlight and FAA charts use. 1 minute of latitude = 1 nm.
  • Degrees, minutes, seconds (N29° 10' 47.6"): used in the Chart Supplement and many official documents.
  • MGRS (17R MN 94350 27918): the military grid. Zone and band, a 100 km square, then easting and northing. 10 digits locate a 1 m square, 8 digits 10 m, 6 digits 100 m.
  • UTM: the metric grid MGRS is built on, as zone, easting and northing in meters.

All conversions use the WGS 84 datum, the same as GPS. MGRS and UTM do not cover the polar areas (north of 84° N or south of 80° S).

You can also paste compact forms like 2910.79N08103.49W or 291048N0810329W.
